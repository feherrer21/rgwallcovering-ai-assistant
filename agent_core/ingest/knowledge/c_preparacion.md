General trade background on what happens to a wall before anything new goes
on it. Whether any of this is included in a given quote or billed separately
is a question for the company doing the work.

## Why preparation matters more than people expect

A wallcovering follows the surface underneath it. Anything left on the wall —
a lump, a seam, a patch of loose paint, a ridge of old adhesive — shows
through the finished result, sometimes more visibly than it did before. Most
finishes that disappoint were installed over a surface that was not ready,
not hung badly.

## What preparation typically involves

**Removing existing covering.** Difficulty varies enormously. Modern
strippable papers can come away cleanly in whole lengths. Older layers,
painted-over wallpaper, or covering applied directly to unsealed drywall can
be slow and can damage the surface as it comes off, creating repair work that
was not visible beforehand. This is the single hardest part of a job to
predict without seeing it.

**Repairing the substrate.** Filling holes and cracks, re-fixing loose areas,
addressing any damp — which needs the cause fixed, not just the symptom, since
moisture will lift a new covering as readily as it lifted the old one.

**Dealing with texture.** Heavily textured surfaces generally need skimming
flat. This is a plastering operation with its own drying time.

**Sealing or priming.** Bare or porous surfaces are usually sealed so that
adhesive behaves predictably and so the covering can be removed cleanly in
future without destroying the wall underneath.

## Newly built or freshly plastered walls

New plaster needs to dry fully and is normally sealed before covering. Hanging
over plaster that has not finished drying tends to fail.

## The practical point

Because preparation depends on what is actually on and under the wall, it is
the part of a project least suited to estimation from a description or a
photograph, and the most common reason a project's real scope differs from
what was expected.
